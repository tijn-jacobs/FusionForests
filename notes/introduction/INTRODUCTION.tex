\section{Introduction}

We estimate heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) in survival outcomes from randomised and observational data.
Precision medicine uses this heterogeneity to guide treatment decisions \citep{hamburg2010path, kent2018personalized}.
Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are the gold standard for causal effect estimation, but they are underpowered for subgroup effects \citep{rothwell2005subgroup} and often have limited follow-up.
Observational or real-world data (RWD) from registries and cohorts are larger and often follow patients for longer.
Regulators increasingly accept them as evidence \citep{sherman2016realworld, fda2018framework}.
Unmeasured confounding can bias treatment effect estimates from observational data.
We identify the effect from the trial and gain power and extended follow-up from the real-world data.
Our motivating example is HIV antiretroviral therapy.
The treatment effect varies with baseline CD4 count, viral load, and age \citep{egger2002prognosis}.
We fuse the ACTG~175 trial \citep{hammer1996} with the Multicenter AIDS Cohort Study \citep{kaslow1987}.
The cohort followed participants over more than 25 years with biannual visits.

The fusion of a trial with real-world data raises three challenges for survival outcomes.
The first is unmeasured confounding in the real-world data.
Treatment is not randomised, and assignment depends on factors the measured covariates do not fully capture.
The no-unmeasured-confounders assumption is untestable and often violated in practice \citep{rosenbaum2002observational,vanderweele2017evalue}.
The second is heterogeneity between the two data sources.
Even when the treatment effect transports across sources, the baseline prognosis often does not.
The trial and observational populations typically differ in eligibility, follow-up, and case mix \citep{stuart2011propensity,degtiar2023review}.
A naive analysis confounds this baseline difference with the treatment effect.
The third is the survival outcome.
Right censoring arises when the event has not occurred by the end of follow-up, so the survival time is known only to exceed the censoring time.
The real-world data may also be interval-censored, with events recorded only at periodic visits.
The choice of estimand on the time scale also requires care.
The hazard ratio is the conventional target, but it is difficult to interpret as a causal effect \citep{hernan2010}.
We adopt the acceleration factor instead, a causally interpretable alternative \citep{Brathovde2024}.

The confounding function measures the bias from unmeasured confounding in observational data.
Early uses cast it as a sensitivity-analysis device within a single study \citep{robins2000sensitivity, yang2018sensitivity, kasza2017assessing}.
\citet{Kallus2018} turn it into an estimation target: they learn the confounding function using the RCT and debias the observational estimate of the treatment effect.
\citet{YangLiuZengWang2025} place this on a semiparametric-efficient footing.
They jointly estimate the treatment effect and the confounding function, and add a test for unmeasured confounding.
The elastic integrative estimator of \citet{yang2023elastic} uses that test to decide whether to borrow from the real-world data, and discards it when confounding is detected.
An R-learner variant \citep{wu2022integrative} and an extension to incomplete real-world data \citep{Yao2024} build on the same approach.
In the survival setting, \citet{Ye2025} work under an accelerated failure time model with right censoring and high-dimensional covariates.
They treat the confounding function as a sparse component selected jointly with the treatment effect.
\citet{mao2025statistical} target the conditional restricted mean survival time under right censoring.


Bayesian methods combine data sources by borrowing information through the prior.
The power prior of \citet{ibrahim2000power} raises the external likelihood to a fractional power that controls the borrowing.
The meta-analytic-predictive (MAP) prior of \citet{neuenschwander2010} makes the borrowing hierarchical and adaptive to between-source heterogeneity.
Later variants refine the idea: robust mixtures guard against prior-data conflict \citep{schmidli2014robust}, commensurate priors tie the borrowing to the between-source discrepancy \citep{hobbs2011, hobbs2012commensurate}, and semiparametric versions replace the Gaussian heterogeneity distribution with a Dirichlet-process mixture \citep{hupf2021bayesian}.
\citet{alt2025control} extend this borrowing to survival data through hierarchical proportional-hazards models.
These methods target a marginal or control-arm effect, not the heterogeneous treatment effect.
A more recent literature targets heterogeneous effects directly. 
\citet{zhou2021incorporating} pool trial and external outcome surfaces with Bayesian additive regression trees, but do not allow for unmeasured confounding in the external source.
\citet{dimitriou2025causal} develop a multi-task Gaussian process with a data-adaptive borrowing parameter.
None of these Bayesian methods targets HTE estimation for survival outcomes under unmeasured confounding.


We develop a Bayesian nonparametric framework for heterogeneous treatment effects on survival outcomes.
It fuses a randomised trial with real-world data without assuming the observational source is unconfounded.
A confounding function absorbs the bias from unmeasured confounding in the real-world treated arm.
We estimate this function rather than assume it, and we keep the real-world data rather than discarding it when confounding is present.
A hierarchical prior handles between-source heterogeneity by borrowing the baseline prognosis across sources and letting it differ between them.
The model accommodates right- and interval-censored data, and exploits the longer follow-up of the real-world source for robustness.
A hierarchical Dirichlet process mixture imposes no parametric form on the error distribution and lets it vary across sources.
